# Docs Writing

Write documentation as product UX, not as storage for facts.

## Anchor On The Real Reader

- Identify the primary reader's role, sophistication, and moment of need.
- Write for the person doing the work, not for an internal team map or a vague
  generic audience.
- Treat secondary readers as handoff audiences, not co-primary audiences.

## Optimize For Confidence And Momentum

- Reduce uncertainty and make the next action feel obvious.
- Prefer writing that helps the reader decide, proceed, or know when to bring
  someone else in.
- Treat sophistication as the feeling that the docs understand what matters.

## Lead With Outcomes, Not Product Taxonomy

- Explain what the reader can achieve and when something should be used.
- Translate internal concepts into the language the audience already uses.
- Keep feature inventories subordinate to practical value.

## Stay Concrete

- Prefer specific examples, concrete nouns, and real workflows.
- Replace abstract claims with what changes for the reader in practice.
- Use screenshots, clips, sample prompts, example requests, or example outputs
  whenever they reduce ambiguity faster than prose.

## Teach Judgment, Not Just Facts

- Help the reader choose between options, not just learn that options exist.
- Include tradeoffs, anti-patterns, and signs that scope is too broad or too
  narrow.
- Good docs compress decision-making, not just information.

## Keep The Tone Calm And Exact

- Use plain, direct language.
- Avoid hype, filler, and vague superlatives.
- Make claims that can survive scrutiny from both operators and reviewers.

## Separate Depth Without Diluting Either Audience

- Keep the main path optimized for the primary reader.
- Route technical depth into focused explanations when it is genuinely needed.
- Do not force non-technical readers through implementation detail just to learn
  the basics.

## Make The Docs Feel Lived In

- Reflect real usage, real constraints, and real handoff points.
- Show what good looks like so the reader can recognize success.
- Prefer realistic examples over idealized demo language.

## Preserve Credibility

- Be explicit about limits, prerequisites, and where human review matters.
- Do not hide complexity; place it where the right reader can use it.
- If the product experience depends on trust, write to earn trust rather than
  to impress.

## Apply The Same Standard To Technical Docs

- Keep reference docs just as audience-aware as product docs.
- Group technical detail around the jobs engineers actually need to do.
- Preserve exactness, but still explain what a route, auth flow, or error model
  is for before listing every detail.

## Check The Draft Before Finalizing

- Can the intended reader quickly tell whether this is for them?
- Will they understand when to use the thing and when not to?
- Does the writing reduce ambiguity, or just rearrange jargon?
- Are there enough examples or proof points to make the guidance believable?
- Does the draft help the reader move forward with confidence?
